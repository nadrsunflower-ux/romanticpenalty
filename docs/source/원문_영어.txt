A B S T R A C T
Female entrepreneurship is increasingly common, yet we know little about how it affects women's 
romantic prospects, a crucial outcome with implications for family formation. We propose that 
female entrepreneurs, particularly those pursuing growth-oriented ventures, face diminished 
romantic prospects, potentially shaped by gendered trait inferences and concerns about time 
demand. Using two field experiments, supplemented by a survey experiment, we provide causal 
evidence that female entrepreneurs are on average penalized in romantic relationship initiation, 
but are preferred by male entrepreneurs. Further analysis offers suggestive evidence on mecha­
nisms underlying this romantic penalty. By examining the impact of female entrepreneurship on 
romantic prospects, our study extends the family embeddedness perspective in entrepreneurship 
research. It also contributes to female entrepreneurship research by documenting a romantic 
penalty as a previously underexplored challenge.
Executive summary
An increasing number of women choose to become entrepreneurs, launching their own businesses instead of working for others. 
Prior research has examined how this choice affects women's personal lives, particularly within the family domain, but has pre­
dominantly focused on married women. Far less attention has been paid to how entrepreneurship shapes family outcomes for single 
women. For them, a key family domain outcome is family formation, for which romantic relationship initiation is a critical preceding 
step. Yet, we know little about how single female entrepreneurs, who disproportionately pursue growth rather than lifestyle-oriented 
ventures, are evaluated by prospective partners at this early stage. Examining this question allows us to focus on early stages preceding 
family formation, thereby offering a life course perspective on how an entrepreneurial identity may shape family-related outcomes.
We expect that female entrepreneurs will be penalized in romantic relationship initiation, exhibiting diminished romantic pros­
pects. Prior research suggests that two related considerations may shape this penalty. First, entrepreneurship remains a masculine 
occupation. As such, female entrepreneurs may violate gender role expectations, leading them to be perceived as lacking feminine 
traits that are viewed as desirable in romantic relationships. Second, entrepreneurship is often perceived as a time-demanding 
occupation, especially for unmarried women running growth-oriented ventures. Concerns about time demand may dissuade men

To test these ideas, we conducted two field experiments on a large online dating platform in China. In the first experiment, we 
created fictitious profiles of female and male entrepreneurs and managers and measured romantic prospects based on responses to 
initial contact messages. We found that female entrepreneurs were significantly less likely to receive responses than comparable female 
managers, whereas no such penalty was observed for men. In the second experiment, we compared a growth-oriented startup founder 
and a lifestyle-oriented florist with two feminine occupations (nurse and teacher). We observed a romantic penalty for the startup 
founder but not for the florist. A supplementary survey experiment provided suggestive evidence that trait-based perceptions, 
particularly lower perceived communality and family orientation, were associated with the observed penalty. Evidence related to time 
demand concerns was less conclusive.
Our study identifies an underexplored challenge that women may face when entering and operating in entrepreneurship. Unlike 
prior research that has mostly focused on disadvantages in the business domain, this study highlights a romantic penalty as a pre­

outcomes for single women at stages preceding family formation. Although we do not directly examine entrepreneurial entry or 
performance, our findings suggest that anticipated penalties in the family domain may influence women's entrepreneurial trajectories. 
In addition, our results offer a potential explanation for earlier observations that entrepreneur husbands are more likely to have 
entrepreneur wives, suggesting that attraction between entrepreneurs may play a role.
1. Introduction
In the last few decades, the rate of female entrepreneurship has increased steadily in both developing and developed countries (Hil 
et al., 2024). Although scholars have long noted that entrepreneurs are embedded in family systems (Aldrich and Cliff, 2003; Hughes 
and Jennings, 2020), the link between entrepreneurial processes and family-level factors is often considered particularly important in 
female entrepreneurship (Brush et al., 2009; Jennings and Brush, 2013). Scholars have examined how female entrepreneurs cope with 
family demands (e.g., Jennings and McDougald, 2007; Shelton, 2006), and how entrepreneurial activities affect their family outcomes 
(e.g., Jennings et al., 2013; Li and Tong, 2023).
Prior studies mostly focus on family outcomes for female entrepreneurs who are married and have children, but have paid scant 
attention to single female entrepreneurs and how entrepreneurship may shape earlier stages in the pathway to family formation. This 
oversight is unfortunate. The original formulation of the family embeddedness perspective by Aldrich and Cliff (2003) links entre­
preneurial activity not only to family-level factors, but specifically to life course transitions, such as marriage and divorce (Aldrich 
et al., 2021; Randerson et al., 2015). A richer understanding of this perspective thus requires exploring how entrepreneurial activity 
may shape processes that precede family formation.
In contemporary societies, family formation is typically preceded by a dating process, in which romantic partners learn about and 
adapt to each other as potential long-term partners (Afifi and Lucas, 2008; Oppenheimer, 1988). Because this learning and adaptation 
process is costly (Becker, 1973), individuals often make careful choices deciding with whom to initiate a romantic relationship, 
especially one that intends to lead to long-term commitments like marriage. In this paper, the likelihood of romantic relationship 
initiation as an early-stage outcome is conceptualized as romantic prospects. We consider whether being an entrepreneur diminishes a 
woman's romantic prospects.
Existing research highlights two related perspectives relevant to this question. First, entrepreneurial research on gender stereo­
typing notes that people frequently associate entrepreneurship with masculinity (Ahl, 2006; Jennings and Tonoyan, 2022), which 
contradicts the feminine traits expected of women. This incompatibility shapes perceptions of female entrepreneurs as less competent 
in the business domain (e.g., Guzman and Kacperczyk, 2019; Kanze et al., 2018). In the family domain, we suggest that this in­
compatibility may also lead female entrepreneurs to be inferred as lacking traits desirable in romantic relationships. A second 
perspective relates to time demand concerns. Social norms in many societies are such that men are expected to specialize in market 
work, whereas women are expected to specialize in housework (e.g., Bianchi et al., 2012; Bittman et al., 2003; He and Noussair, 2023). 
Because entrepreneurship offers increased returns to longer work hours (Goldin, 2014), female entrepreneurs may be perceived as less 
willing or able to commit to family responsibilities, thus violating gendered expectations of intra-household division of labor. 
Together, trait inference and time demand perspectives suggest that female entrepreneurs may face reduced romantic prospects.
Empirically demonstrating the causal impact of female entrepreneurship on romantic prospects is challenging using observational 
data. To address this challenge, following prior research (e.g., Egebark et al., 2021; Ong and Wang, 2015), we conducted field ex­
periments on one of the largest dating websites in China. We found that female, but not male, entrepreneurs were penalized in 
romantic relationship initiation, although mutual attraction between male and female entrepreneurs was also observed. Through a 
survey experiment, we explored the underlying mechanisms. While evidence is only suggestive, we find an explanation based on trait 
inference to be more compelling, whereas evidence related to time demand concerns is more ambiguous.
This study makes three contributions. First, it broadens entrepreneurship research grounded in the family embeddedness 
perspective. Because this perspective is rooted in a life course model marked by key transition events (e.g., marriage, divorce), a fuller 
understanding requires examining different life stages of entrepreneurs (Aldrich et al., 2021). By focusing on the romantic prospects of 
unmarried female entrepreneurs, we extend this perspective to an understudied stage—romantic relationship initiation—preceding 
family formation. Second, this study advances understanding of the origins of entrepreneurial families. Prior research documents the 
disproportionate prevalence of entrepreneur households, in which both the husband and wife are self-employed (Parker, 2008). Such

2. Theory and hypotheses
2.1. Female entrepreneurship and its link to the family domain
The family embeddedness perspective of entrepreneurship has long emphasized the inextricable connection between entrepre­
neurial activity and family processes (Aldrich and Cliff, 2003). Prior research highlights the bidirectional nature of this connection: 
entrepreneurial activity is not only influenced by, but also influences, family-level factors (Aldrich and Cliff, 2003; Jennings et al., 
2013). Although not gender-specific, this perspective is closely related to and partly owes its origin to female entrepreneurship 
research (Jennings et al., 2013). Brush and colleagues, for instance, explicitly place “motherhood” at the center of their gender-aware 
framework of female entrepreneurship (Brush et al., 2009), where motherhood serves as a metaphor for the family context.
In this vein, extensive research has documented that family-level factors, especially childcare responsibilities, are a key driver of 
female entrepreneurship (e.g., Carr, 1996; Noseleit, 2014; Semykina, 2018; Th´ebaud, 2015). Meanwhile, scholars have called for more 
research examining how entrepreneurial activity, in turn, affects family outcomes for women (Jennings et al., 2013). Extant research 
has shown how female entrepreneurs manage work-family conflict at the business-family interface (e.g., Eddleston and Powell, 2012; 
Jennings and McDougald, 2007; Parasuraman and Simmers, 2001; Shelton, 2006), as well as how female entrepreneurship affects 
other aspects of family life, such as time allocation (Jurik, 1998; Loscocco and Bird, 2012) and childcare quality (Li and Tong, 2023; 
Schindehutte et al., 2003).
With few exceptions, however, this body of work focuses predominantly on female entrepreneurs who are married and have 
children. Although this emphasis aligns with the core premise of the family embeddedness perspective, it also narrows our under­
standing of how entrepreneurial activity intersects with family processes across different life stages. In its original formulation, the 
family embeddedness perspective adopts a life course approach that explicitly links entrepreneurial activity to key transition events, 
such as marriage and divorce (Aldrich et al., 2021). A fuller understanding of this perspective therefore requires examining how 
entrepreneurial identity may shape women's family-related outcomes more broadly, including those that precede family formation.
Although not all female entrepreneurs are concerned about marriage, qualitative evidence suggests that at least some, particularly 
in contexts such as China, face pressure to marry (Song and Li, 2023). More generally, research shows that women often consider 
anticipated family outcomes when making career choices (Bursztyn et al., 2020; Zhang and Zou, 2023). Taken together, these insights 
highlight the importance of examining how female entrepreneurship may shape women's romantic outcomes prior to family 
formation.
2.2. Female entrepreneurship and romantic prospects
In modern societies, marriage is often the end result of a romantic relationship that progresses through several stages (e.g., Ker­
ckhoff and Davis, 1962; Murstein, 1970; Regan, 2011), the earliest of which is relationship initiation—the decision to pursue a 
romantic relationship with a potential partner (Afifi and Lucas, 2008; Fisman et al., 2006; Lewis, 2016). This decision is costly because 
initiating a romantic relationship requires commitment to learning about and adapting to a chosen partner (Afifi and Lucas, 2008; 
Oppenheimer, 1988). As such, individuals are more likely to initiate a romantic relationship when a partner appears to be a promising 
long-term candidate. This choice becomes even more deliberate with the rise of online dating platforms (e.g., Lewis, 2016; Sprecher, 
2009), where individuals must make explicit and immediate choice decisions.
Rather than examining marriage formation directly, we focus on how female entrepreneurs are evaluated at the stage of romantic 
relationship initiation. We refer to the likelihood of initiating a romantic relationship as romantic prospects. Because of high infor­
mation asymmetry prior to relationship initiation, people frequently rely on observable cues to infer candidate quality. Occupation is 
one such important cue (Oppenheimer, 1988). Entrepreneurship is highly heterogeneous (e.g., Budig, 2006; Kanze et al., 2020; 
Tonoyan et al., 2010; Tonoyan and Strohmeyer, 2021). In this study, we focus on a career-driven, growth-oriented conception of 
female entrepreneurship, which is often characteristic of single female entrepreneurs (Patrick et al., 2016) and is particularly relevant 
in the context of romantic relationship initiation.
Existing literature points to two related theoretical perspectives through which women's romantic prospects may be affected by 
their entrepreneur status. These perspectives are not mutually exclusive and may operate simultaneously in shaping early-stage 
romantic evaluations. One perspective emphasizes gender stereotyping and role incongruity. Entrepreneurship remains a largely 
masculine occupation: people tend to associate it with agentic traits such as ambitiousness, assertiveness, and confidence (Ahl, 2006). 
Even when compared to managers, entrepreneurs are still seen as comparatively more masculine (Baron et al., 2001; Raile et al., 2024). 
Moreover, although the manager role has become gradually associated with more feminine traits over the past few decades (Duehr and 
Bono, 2006; Powell et al., 2021), this pattern is not observed in entrepreneurship (Raile et al., 2024). The masculine job role conflicts 
with the female gender role, in which communal traits such as being nurturing and sympathetic are expected (Heilman et al., 1989),

resulting in role incongruity (Eagly, 1987).
Role incongruity results in two forms of prejudice (Eagly and Karau, 2002). The first arises when people infer that women lack the 
traits and skills required to succeed in masculine occupations (Cash et al., 1977; Gupta et al., 2009; Heilman and Stopeck, 1985), a 
process termed job role violation (Johnson et al., 2014). In the entrepreneurship context, substantial evidence shows that female 
entrepreneurs face greater difficulty acquiring financial and human resources (e.g., Bigelow et al., 2014; Brooks et al., 2014; Guzman 
and Kacperczyk, 2019; Jennings and Tonoyan, 2022; Lim and Suh, 2019; Liu et al., 2024; Poczter and Shapsis, 2018; Tonoyan et al., 
2025). Providing direct experimental evidence, Tonoyan et al. (2025) show that female technology entrepreneurs are perceived by job 
seekers as less competent and less agentic than otherwise comparable male entrepreneurs.
The second form concerns women's conformity to the female gender role. This gender role violation may lead men to infer a lack of 
communal traits normally expected of women (Heilman and Okimoto, 2007), and even to infer the presence of counter-communal 
traits (Heilman et al., 1989; Heilman et al., 1995). Consistently, women politicians are less preferred by voters when they exhibit 
masculine qualities (Okimoto and Brescoll, 2010), and female managers are frequently viewed as less likable and less desirable as 
leaders (Heilman et al., 1995; Heilman and Okimoto, 2007). Similarly, experimental evidence shows that female technology entre­
preneurs are seen as less warm than their male counterparts by job seekers (Tonoyan et al., 2025). Prior research directly comparing 
managers and entrepreneurs finds that female entrepreneurs are perceived as less feminine, more decisive, and more assertive than 
managers (Baron et al., 2001).
Although prior research shows that gender role violation affects women's professional outcomes, it may also extend to the family 
domain. To the extent that female entrepreneurs are inferred as lacking communal traits, their romantic prospects may be diminished. 
Mate choice research shows that both men and women value communal attributes in romantic partners, especially in long-term re­
lationships (Buss and Barnes, 1986; Kenrick et al., 1990; Regan et al., 2000). This line of reasoning suggests that female entrepreneurs 
may face greater difficulty at the stage of romantic relationship initiation if they are perceived as lacking traits desirable for long-term 
partnership.
In addition, prior research highlights the expectation that women shoulder household chores, especially responsibilities related to 
child and family care. A prominent explanation for this (expected) intra-household division of labor is based on comparative 
advantage: men are assumed to be more productive in the labor market, making it rational for women to specialize in housework 
(Becker, 1973). However, this efficiency-based explanation has been challenged by subsequent research (e.g., Bianchi et al., 2012; 
Bittman et al., 2003; Hook, 2010; Raley et al., 2012). In particular, social norms play a crucial role in shaping intra-household division 
of labor. Studies show that people—including women themselves—expect women to handle household chores even in the absence of 
productivity differences, such as when tasks are merely framed as housework or when gendered norms are made salient (e.g., Badgett 
and Folbre, 1999; He and Noussair, 2023; Roncolato and Roomets, 2020). Despite some change and variation, these expectations and 
norms remain persistent over time and across nations (Bianchi et al., 2012).
These expectations can create challenges for female entrepreneurs. Male-dominated occupations—entrepreneurship being a 
prominent example—are often time demanding, with pay exhibiting increasing marginal returns to hours worked (Goldin, 2014). This 
pay-work hour profile may discourage women from selecting into such occupations, reinforcing their association with masculinity. At 
the same time, for women who enter these occupations, such as female entrepreneurs, concerns about work time demand and limited 
availability for family responsibilities may arise. To the extent that such time demand concerns arise, men may be reluctant to initiate 
romantic relationships with female entrepreneurs who appear less available for family responsibilities.
We do not expect male entrepreneurs to face diminished romantic prospects. First, because male entrepreneurs occupy a gender- 
typical role, they are unlikely to experience gender role violation or negative trait inferences. Second, although male entrepreneurs 
may also raise time demand concerns, prevailing social norms make such concerns less likely to incur substantial penalties (Bianchi 
et al., 2012; He and Noussair, 2023; Roncolato and Roomets, 2020). Taken together, these perspectives suggest that female, but not 
male, entrepreneurs may be evaluated less favorably in romantic relationship initiation contexts. 
Hypothesis 1.
(H1). Compared with women in gender-typical occupations, female entrepreneurs will face reduced romantic 
prospects.
While we have proposed that female entrepreneurs are, on average, penalized in seeking a romantic relationship, the arguments 
developed above suggest that the extent of this penalty depends on men's perceptions when evaluating potential partners. Here, we 
consider a particular source of heterogeneity in these perceptions: men's own occupation, specifically whether they are entrepreneurs 
themselves.
Homophily is a fundamental feature in social interactions (McPherson et al., 2001). It is a pattern in which people prefer to 
associate with similar others and is observed across numerous settings (e.g., Bengtsson and Hsu, 2015; Freeman and Huang, 2015; 
Gompers et al., 2016). In romantic relationships, homophily manifests in homogamy: people tend to prefer partners with similar 
education levels, ethnic or cultural background, and socio-economic status (e.g., Kalmijn, 1994, 1998). Homophily also leads to 
occupational homogamy, such that men and women from the same occupation are more likely to be romantically involved (Choi and 
Qian, 2023; Hayes, 1993).
Occupational homogamy can be explained by search and matching on socioeconomic and cultural resources (Kalmijn, 1998). 
Individuals tend to compete for romantic partners with greater socioeconomic resources, defined as resources that generate economic 
well-being and status. This preference arises because married couples typically pool their resources to maximize household income and 
status. Due to wage differentials across occupations, this competition results in individuals sorting within their own occupations 
(Schwartz et al., 2021; Schwartz, 2013).
Occupational homogamy is further strengthened by the inherent uncertainty in evaluating a romantic partner's socioeconomic

better access to information and greater ability to evaluate each other's future prospects. Because entrepreneurs often exhibit riskier 
income profiles, uncertainty in evaluating their socioeconomic resources may be especially high, which may further reinforce occu­

Occupational homogamy also arises because people prefer romantic partners with similar cultural resources, including values and 
attitudes. Cultural similarity enables individuals to develop a shared lifestyle, resulting in greater social confirmation and affection 
(Kalmijn, 1994), and is also a prerequisite for relationship initiation (Kalmijn, 1998). Prior research shows that cultural resources are 
unevenly distributed across occupations (Knafo and Sagiv, 2004; Sagiv et al., 2017), partly because occupational entry screens in­

in that occupation (Kohn and Schooler, 1969; Kohn, 1989; Mortimer, 1976). As a result, individuals in the same occupation are more 
likely to exhibit high cultural similarity.
Entrepreneurs tend to have distinctive values and attitudes, particularly those related to individualism (e.g., Bazzi et al., 2020; 
Mostaghel et al., 2022). For instance, entrepreneurs tend to value freedom (Fagenson, 1993), self-direction and non-conformity (Holt, 
1997), and self-reliance (Beugelsdijk and Noorderhaven, 2005) more than comparable managers or the general population. More 
importantly, the values of (male and female) entrepreneurs differ significantly from those of managers (Fagenson, 1993). Sharing these 
distinctive cultural resources may promote mutual attraction between female and male entrepreneurs while making cross- 
occupational matching less likely.
Overall, occupational homogamy may help offset the romantic penalty faced by female entrepreneurs by increasing their desir­
ability among male entrepreneurs who share similar occupational backgrounds. 
Hypothesis 2.
(H2). The reduction in romantic prospects will be weaker for female entrepreneurs evaluated by male entrepreneurs.
3. Methods
3.1. Research design
Estimating the causal impact of female entrepreneurship on romantic prospects poses significant inference challenges. Women do 
not enter entrepreneurship randomly. Those with certain personality traits (Baum et al., 2014; Cassar, 2010; Zhao et al., 2010) and 
specific preferences and attitudes (Carr, 1996) are more likely to self-select into entrepreneurship. These same factors may simulta­
neously influence how women behave and are perceived in romantic contexts (Parker, 2008). When unaccounted for, such factors may 
bias estimation results or, in the extreme, render any observed relationship between female entrepreneurship and romantic prospects 
spurious. Unfortunately, fully controlling these factors is often infeasible.
Given this empirical challenge, the ideal research design would involve randomly assigning women to entrepreneur and non- 
entrepreneur status and then observing how they are evaluated in romantic relationship initiation. With this experimental ideal in 
mind, we conducted two field experiments on one of the largest online dating websites in China.2 Field experiments on online dating 
platforms are particularly well suited for identifying causal effects in early-stage romantic interactions (e.g., Egebark et al., 2021; 
Huber and Malhotra, 2017; Lee and Niederle, 2015; Ong, 2016; Ong and Wang, 2015), as online dating has become an increasingly 
popular venue for initiating romantic relationships (Egebark et al., 2021; Huber and Malhotra, 2017; Qian et al., 2022), including 
relationships oriented toward long-term partnership.
We chose this particular website because it is positioned as a serious, long-term relationship-oriented platform, making it more 
likely to attract individuals seeking long-term commitments. Although individuals interested in casual dating may also appear on the 
platform, they are less likely to be incentivized to do so, as platforms marketed for casual dating would constitute more attractive 
alternatives. The website's large user base ensured that we could collect sufficient data. Moreover, its relatively simple registration 
process made it feasible to implement our experimental manipulations.3 Both field experiments received IRB approval.
3.1.1. Design for field experiment 1
In the first field experiment, we seek to establish a baseline causal estimate of the effect of female entrepreneurship on romantic 
prospects at the stage of relationship initiation. We began by creating a prototype, a fictitious female user profile. Following prior work 
(Ong, 2016), we designed the profile with above-average demographic and financial characteristics to ensure sufficient interest. 
Demographically, we used an AI-generated portrait as the profile photo, which was rated by a group of business students to be above 
average in attractiveness. The profile age was set to 26, two years younger than the average age of first-time marriage for women in 
China based on 2020 statistics. Height was set at 165 cm, five centimeters above the average height of women aged 25–29, also based 
on 2020 statistics. For financial status, the website provided five income brackets. We selected the second highest bracket (i.e.,

which in Chinese carries the connotation of a more serious business rather than a lifestyle business. Personal bios were displayed 
prominently at the top center of the profile page.
Because online dating tends to occur locally, we created profiles in both Shanghai and Xi'an. Shanghai is a highly global and 
industrialized city where residents may hold less rigid and conventional gender role expectations (Xiao and Qian, 2020), whereas Xi'an 
is a large but more traditional inland city. This dual-city setup increases contextual diversity and allows us to examine whether effects 
differ across socio-cultural contexts.
To assess whether any observed penalty was gender-specific, we also created fictitious male profiles following the same procedures. 
This resulted in eight profiles (Entrepreneur/Manager × Shanghai/Xi'an × Female/Male). Across profiles, the same female or male 
portrait was used to minimize potential differences attributable to appearance. To build the analytical sample, we used the platform's 
search function to identify users of the opposite sex residing in the same city as each fictitious profile. We then sent a contact message to 
a subset of these users. The message read:
“I read your profile information and wish to know more about you. If you are interested, please reply.”
We sorted search results and contacted users who were most recently active,5 to ensure sent messages were read promptly and to 
minimize the inclusion of dormant accounts. Between April and December 2022, 200 messages were sent from each of the eight 
profiles, reaching 1600 unique recipients in total.6 After excluding observations with missing key recipient information (e.g., occu­
pation), the final sample size was 1408.7
3.1.2. Design for field experiment 2
So far, we have presumed an image of female entrepreneurs being highly career-driven and growth-oriented, which is characteristic 
of many single female entrepreneurs (Patrick et al., 2016), and used managers as a comparison group. However, it is unclear whether 
the same penalty extends to lifestyle entrepreneurs, and whether the penalty remains when using more feminine occupations as the 
comparison group. These considerations motivate our second field experiment.
Specifically, we developed two entrepreneur profiles and two control occupations. One entrepreneur profile was a founder of a 
technology startup in the cybersecurity industry (startup founder), representing a typically masculine, growth-oriented entrepreneur 
(Tonoyan et al., 2025).8 In contrast, the other entrepreneur profile was a flower shop owner (florist), an occupation often seen as 
feminine and more lifestyle-oriented. Unlike growth-oriented entrepreneurs, lifestyle entrepreneurs are primarily motivated by 
intrinsic goals, such as personal fulfilment, and emphasize work-life integration over wealth maximization (Ivanycheva et al., 2024). 
They often choose to limit business growth to preserve time for lifestyle activities and maintain compatibility between work and leisure 
(O'Neill et al., 2022). As a result, lifestyle female entrepreneurs may be perceived as less agentic and as facing more flexible and less 
intensive work time demands, potentially mitigating perceived role incongruity and time demand concerns, and the associated penalty 
in romantic relationship initiation. For the two control occupations, we used a nurse and a primary school Chinese teacher, both 
quintessential feminine occupations (Shinar, 1975; White et al., 1989). Nurses tend to face irregular and long work hours, especially in 
China (Li et al., 2024), and therefore represent a highly time demanding occupation. Primary school teachers, in contrast, have 
relatively predictable and reasonable work schedules, with summer and winter vacations.

Apart from these changes, the remaining design elements mirrored those of the first experiment. The same demographic infor­
mation and portrait were used across all four profiles. Occupations were again disclosed in the personal bio and the drop-down menu. 
In July and August 2025, our research team sent the same contact message as in the first experiment to male website users in Xi'an. To 
avoid any recipient receiving more than one message, we sorted male users by their most recent activity date and sent messages in 
rotation across the four profiles. To maintain consistency with the first experiment, 200 contact messages were sent from each of the 
four profiles, reaching 800 unique recipients in total. After excluding observations with missing key recipient information (e.g., 
occupation), the final sample size was 787.9
3.2. Measures
Following prior research (e.g., Egebark et al., 2021; Huber and Malhotra, 2017; Lee and Niederle, 2015; Ong, 2016; Ong and Wang, 
2015), we used whether a recipient replied to the contact message (Response) as our main outcome measure. A reply represents the 
earliest observable step toward romantic relationship initiation and thus reflects romantic prospects in our context. Messages that were 
read but not replied to within two weeks were treated as indicating non-interest and coded as 0 for Response. In contrast, a replied 
message was coded as 1.10
We used Entrepreneur Sender to denote entrepreneur profiles in both experiments. In Field Experiment 1, this variable was coded as 
1 for the entrepreneur profile and 0 for the manager profile. The effects of Female Entrepreneur Sender and Male Entrepreneur Sender 
were also examined separately in Field Experiment 1. In Field Experiment 2, Entrepreneur Sender was coded as 1 for the cybersecurity 
startup founder and the florist, and 0 for the remaining profiles. We also examined these profiles separately.
We hypothesized that the impact of female entrepreneur status on response depends on whether the message recipient is an 
entrepreneur. This moderator, Entrepreneur Recipient, was constructed based on recipient profile information and coded as 1 if the 
recipient listed “operates own business” and 0 otherwise.
Our experimental design ensures randomization of the entrepreneur status, which allows us to derive unbiased causal estimates. To 
improve estimate precision, we collected a set of control variables to account for recipients' personal attributes. In the assortative 
mating literature, age, education level, and income are among the most important factors affecting partner selection (e.g., Schwartz, 
2013). Accordingly, we controlled for the recipient's reported Age. Education was measured using a categorical variable coded as 
0 (below high school), 1 (high school graduate), 2 (college graduate), 3 (master's degree), and 4 (doctoral degree). The website allowed 
users to self-report monthly income using five bracket levels (with higher values indicating higher income), which we used to construct 
the Income variable. We also included binary indicators for whether recipients had Never Married, owned a House or a Car, or resided in 
Shanghai rather than Xi'an (Field Experiment 1 only). Finally, we controlled for whether the recipient had a local (Shanghai or Xi'an) 
Hukou,11 which significantly affects long-term partner prospects in China (Qian and Qian, 2017).
Given our experimental design, we conducted response-level analyses to examine how sender and recipient characteristics—most 
importantly whether the message sender was a female entrepreneur—affected the likelihood of receiving a response. In addition to the 
control variables described above, we included month fixed effects for when the message was sent.

4.3. Supplementary survey experiment
We supplemented our field experiments with a survey experiment. Although a survey experiment presents hypothetical choice 
scenarios and lacks the realism of field settings, it has two advantages. First, it allows us to replicate prior findings and assess their 
robustness at a relatively low cost. In particular, we used the same female portrait in both field experiments. Although this choice 
reduced noise associated with variations in facial appearance, it risked limiting generalizability. The survey setting allows us to address 
this concern by including multiple female portraits. We therefore employ stimulus sampling by presenting participants with varied 
stimuli (i.e., portraits) rather than a single stimulus (Cummings and Reeves, 2022; Wells and Windschitl, 1999).15 This practice has 
also been used in other entrepreneurship studies that rely on portraits (e.g., Tonoyan et al., 2025; Younkin and Kuppuswamy, 2019). 
Moreover, in the field experiments, the only feasible outcome was whether a recipient responded. The survey experiment allows us to 
examine more nuanced outcomes. Second, the survey experiment allows us to explore how respondents perceive traits related to 
gender role and time demand, and how these perceptions co-vary with their evaluations of female entrepreneurs. Relatedly, we also 
aimed to collect qualitative information on men's opinions about female entrepreneurs to gain additional insight into underlying 
perceptions.
The survey was designed to mimic what male respondents would experience in a field setting. They were asked to imagine that they 
had registered on a serious, long-term relationship-oriented dating website and had received a contact message from a female user. The 
female profile was then displayed. To reduce cognitive burden, the profile contained only key information: a portrait, occupation, 
income, education level, and age. Using an AI-powered image database provided by Generated Photos,16 we selected an initial set of 35 
Asian female portraits. The authors then iteratively removed highly similar portraits (e.g., minor variations of others), resulting in 13 
distinct portraits.
For occupations, we used either a technology startup founder in the cybersecurity industry or a primary school Chinese teacher. 
Income and age were set to match those used in the field experiments. This design produced 26 profiles, one of which was randomly 
assigned to each respondent. After viewing the profile, respondents were asked whether they would be willing to (1) respond to the 
message, which required registering for a membership costing 15 Yuan per month (Response),17 and, conditional on responding, (2) 
meet the female user offline, such as by having dinner together (Meet).

We then included several perception-based measures related to gender role traits and time demand. Gender role violation may 
trigger trait inferences, leading men to perceive female entrepreneurs as lower in communality and higher in agency (e.g., Heilman 
et al., 1989; Heilman et al., 1995). Because we focus on perceptions in a romantic context, we used general scales measuring perceived 
Communality (four items, α = 0.87) and Agency (six items, α = 0.86) (Heilman and Okimoto, 2007). We also included a measure of 
Family Orientation (three items, α = 0.71), which captures whether women are seen as understanding, wanting children, and religious, 
attributes prior research suggests are valued in long-term romantic partners (Kenrick et al., 1990). To assess time demand concerns, 
respondents indicated on a 5-point scale how occupied by work they believed the female user to be (Time Demand).
In addition, we measured respondents' Gender Role Attitude using an 8-item scale (Koo et al., 2020) (α = 0.82), which may moderate 
the main effect. We also collected respondent Age, Education, Income, marital status (Never Married), and occupation as controls. At the 
end of the survey, respondents were asked to describe perceived advantages and disadvantages of the female user as a potential long- 
term romantic partner. These questions allowed us to gather qualitative insights. The survey experiment received IRB approval.
The survey was administered on Tencent Questionnaire, a major online survey platform in China with a large and diverse 
respondent pool. We required respondents to be male and over the age of 18. After excluding those who failed attention checks, we 
obtained a total of 985 valid responses. The probability of choosing to respond (73%) was substantially higher than in the field ex­
periments, likely because the hypothetical setting reduced real response costs. Nonetheless, willingness to meet offline was signifi­
cantly lower (31%). Survey respondents were similar in age to those in the field experiments but reported lower average income.18
Table 6 reports the results from the survey experiment. Using whether respondents chose to respond to the message as the outcome 
variable (i.e., consistent with the field experiments), Model 15 replicates our prior finding of a romantic penalty for female entre­
preneurs (β −0.35, SE = 0.14, p < .05). Because the survey profiles contained 13 distinct portraits, this suggests that the romantic 
penalty is not driven by idiosyncratic features of a single portrait. When using willingness to meet offline as the outcome variable, the 
coefficient becomes statistically insignificant (unreported). This pattern suggests that female entrepreneur status may act as an initial 
filter: it affects whether men choose to respond, but does not significantly influence the subsequent decision to meet offline. Given this 
finding, subsequent analyses focus on willingness to respond as the outcome.
Controlling for portrait fixed effects has little impact on the point estimate (Model 16), nor does adding control variables (Model 
17). Because the survey draws respondents from diverse provinces in China, Model 18 further includes province fixed effects. The main 
effect becomes smaller in magnitude, consistent with our earlier observation that the magnitude of the romantic penalty varies across 
regions, potentially reflecting differences in underlying values and attitudes. To examine this more directly, Model 19 interacts female 
entrepreneur status with respondents' Gender Role Attitude. The interaction term is statistically significant (β = −0.53, SE = 0.22, p <
.05), suggesting that the romantic penalty for female entrepreneurs is stronger among men who hold more traditional gender role 
attitudes.
Following prior research (e.g., Calic and Mosakowski, 2016), we conducted a mediation analysis by first estimating the effect of the 
explanatory variable on the proposed mediators, followed by the more formal KHB approach (Kohler et al., 2011). The KHB approach 
offers several advantages and has been widely used in entrepreneurship research (e.g., Calic and Mosakowski, 2016; Tonoyan et al., 
2020; Tonoyan et al., 2025). These results are reported in Table 7.
We find that female entrepreneurs are perceived as less communal and more agentic, consistent with predictions based on gender 
role violation. Moreover, men tend to believe that female entrepreneurs are more occupied by work (relative to female teachers), 
consistent with the time demand argument. Female entrepreneurs are also seen as less family oriented. The last column of Table 7
presents the KHB results. These findings suggest that the lower likelihood of responding to female entrepreneurs is partly associated 
with men's perceptions that female entrepreneurs are lower in communality and, marginally, lower in family orientation. Specifically, 
perceived lower communality and lower family orientation are associated with 30.35% and 19.38%, respectively, of the observed 
differential in response likelihood. Although female entrepreneurs are perceived as more agentic and more occupied by work, these 
perceptions do not appear to significantly influence men's willingness to respond.
In addition, we analyzed the open-ended text responses using a BERT-based topic modeling approach.19 The resulting themes 
reveal patterns indicative of trait inference in line with gender role violation (and gender role congruity, in the case of female teachers). 
Male respondents most often cited an “independent personality” and an “accomplished career” as advantages of female entrepreneurs, 
whereas phrases reflecting “domineering” appeared with striking frequency (248 occurrences), making this one of the most frequently 
inferred disadvantages. In contrast, female teachers were frequently described as understanding and kind, as well as family and child 
oriented. For female entrepreneurs, the former quality was rarely mentioned, and the latter was not mentioned. Although some men 
also described teachers as domineering, related phrases appeared much less often (112 occurrences) and often reflected teachers' 
authoritative classroom role rather than innate personality traits.
Both occupations were viewed as time demanding, but a closer examination of responses revealed two nuanced differences. First, 
men explicitly suggested that female entrepreneurs were unable or unwilling to devote time to family. Such concerns were not 
expressed for female teachers. Instead, men frequently noted that teachers were family and child oriented and would have ample free 
time once the school day is over. Second, female teachers were often described as involuntarily occupied by work, whereas female 
entrepreneurs were portrayed as voluntarily and willingly prioritizing work, suggesting that inferences about time demand may be 
intertwined with trait-based inferences.20

5. Discussion and conclusion
This study provides causal evidence that female entrepreneurs face unfavorable evaluation in early-stage romantic relationship 
initiation. It further offers exploratory and suggestive evidence regarding the perceptual processes associated with this penalty. Using 
two field experiments conducted in China, we identify a causal effect of entrepreneur status on men's initial response behavior in an 
online dating setting. In the first field experiment, female entrepreneurs receive fewer responses compared to comparable female 
managers. Extending this finding, the second field experiment shows the romantic penalty is mainly experienced by growth-oriented 
female entrepreneurs (technology startup founders), but not lifestyle-oriented ones (florists). The supplementary survey experiment 
produces consistent results. Because male entrepreneurs, who tend to respond favorably to female entrepreneurs, comprise a small 
share of the dating pool, female entrepreneurs likely face a romantic penalty overall.
We proposed gender role violation (and the resulting trait inferences) and time demand concerns as two potential channels through 
which romantic penalty may arise. Supporting evidence, though suggestive, can be found. In the survey experiment, growth-oriented 
female entrepreneurs are perceived as significantly more agentic and less communal, with lower perceived communality associated 
with a lower willingness to respond. These perceptions are echoed in open-ended responses, where terms such as “domineering,” 
reflecting a combination of high agency and low communality, emerge as prominent negative themes.21 These findings appear to lend 
support to the trait inference mechanism. We also find some support for time demand concerns. Compared to teachers, female en­
trepreneurs are significantly more likely to be seen as occupied by work.
At the same time, our reading of qualitative responses suggests that time demand perceptions often concern women's willingness, 
rather than ability as constrained by objective work demand, to allocate time to family. Specifically, female entrepreneurs are 
frequently portrayed by men as willingly prioritizing work over family, whereas teachers are described as willing to care for family, 
despite being occupied by work. We interpret this pattern as suggesting time demand concerns may be intertwined with or reflect trait- 
based inferences. Despite these interesting findings, we caution that discussions on specific mechanisms that drive the romantic 
penalty are not conclusive. The mechanisms proposed in this study entail perceptual variables that can be interrelated and difficult to 
be cleanly identified. Relatedly, occupational labels bundle multiple attributes, including income expectations, sector stereotypes, 
caregiving norms, perceived flexibility, and social class cues. As a result, our occupational contrasts should be interpreted as composite 
treatments rather than clean tests of any single theoretical account. Accordingly, we interpret our findings as documenting patterned 
responses and co-moving perceptions in early-stage partner evaluation, rather than as definitive adjudication among competing 
explanations.

By examining romantic prospects, we offer an alternative perspective on existing accounts of “entrepreneur households,” or the 
frequent emergence of “copreneurs” (Mahanti et al., 2025). Based on archival data, Parker's (2008) analysis suggests that knowledge 
spillovers between spouses help explain the emergence of such households. Complementing this account, our study provides sug­
gestive evidence that romantic relationship initiation patterns may also contribute to the sorting of male and female entrepreneurs into 
partnerships. More broadly, our findings suggest that examinations of the business-family interface among married entrepreneurs 
should consider potential selection effects occurring at the dating stage.
Our study is also relevant for the female entrepreneurship literature. A major interest in this literature is understanding why female 
entrepreneurs are underrepresented and underperform relative to their male counterparts (Loscocco and Bird, 2012). Both supply and 
demand side explanations have been developed. On the supply side, women often lack the experience and confidence needed to enter 
entrepreneurship (e.g., Langowitz and Minniti, 2007; Th´ebaud, 2010; Tonoyan et al., 2020). On the demand side, female entrepreneurs 
face greater difficulty obtaining investment or must do so at higher cost (e.g., Bigelow et al., 2014; Brooks et al., 2014; Guzman and 
Kacperczyk, 2019; Liu et al., 2024; Muravyev et al., 2009; Poczter and Shapsis, 2018). These findings imply that demand-side 
discrimination within the business domain discourages and disadvantages female entrepreneurship.
Our study extends this literature by moving into the family domain and identifying a romantic penalty as a previously underex­
plored challenge female entrepreneurs may face. In discussions of demand side discrimination in the business domain, scholars 
highlight trait inferences triggered by job role violation as a key explanatory mechanism. Our study provides suggestive evidence that 
gender role violation may also trigger trait inferences that penalize female entrepreneurs in the family domain. Beyond trait inferences, 
we find tentative evidence that concerns about time demand may also reduce female entrepreneurs' romantic prospects.
Taken together, these findings provide a plausible complementary perspective on the underrepresentation and underperformance 
of female entrepreneurship. Although we do not establish a direct link between romantic penalty and women's entry into or perfor­
mance in entrepreneurship, such a connection is theoretically consistent with prior research. Gupta et al. (2009) note that gender 
stereotypes may deter female entrepreneurship by shaping not women's own perceptions, but rather the perceptions of those around 
them (e.g., families), who may in turn discourage women from starting businesses. Relatedly, research on occupational choice suggests 
that women incorporate family considerations, including romantic prospects, into career decisions (McClintock, 2020). For instance, 
women may be less likely to enter occupations associated with delayed marriage (Zhang and Zou, 2023) or downplay career ambitions 
to improve romantic prospects (Bursztyn et al., 2020). Interviews with female entrepreneurs in China further indicate that social 
pressure to marry can shape both personal and professional decisions (Song and Li, 2023). Together, these studies suggest that 
romantic penalty may represent an additional demand side constraint—located in the family domain—shaping women's entrepre­
neurial trajectories.
5.2. Practical implications
Our findings suggest that women considering entrepreneurship as a career path may, on average, encounter an early-stage romantic 
penalty in relationship initiation, in addition to the well-documented challenges in the business domain (e.g., fundraising). Although 
the observed effects are modest and concentrated in initial screening contexts, they indicate that occupational identity can shape 
perceptions beyond the workplace. Awareness of these dynamics may help prospective female entrepreneurs anticipate how entre­
preneur status is interpreted in social settings.
We also observe that male entrepreneurs respond more favorably to female entrepreneurs, suggesting that occupational similarity 
may mitigate some of the observed disadvantage. More broadly, our findings highlight how gendered perceptions of work and family 
roles can extend into personal domains. Efforts to normalize shared caregiving responsibilities, recognize women's contributions to 
both family and professional domains (Goldin, 2021), and promote more gender-neutral images of entrepreneurship may gradually 
reduce such perception-based disparities.
5.3. Limitations and future research
There are several limitations that warrant attention. First, field experiments allow us to identify causal effects in a realistic setting, 
yet they are constrained by feasibility and external validity (Chatterji et al., 2016). We use user reply as a feasible proxy for early-stage 
romantic prospects, forgoing outcomes that may be theoretically desirable but practically difficult to observe or implement (e.g., 
sustained conversations, offline meetings, or long-term relationship formation). Because entrepreneur status does not appear to in­
fluence willingness to meet offline, our findings are best interpreted as applying to the initial screening stage of romantic interaction 
rather than to later relationship development. Profile characteristics (e.g., age, income) were selected to generate sufficient engage­
ment, consistent with prior research. These design choices may limit ecological realism to the extent that respondents hold different 
baseline expectations about entrepreneurs' age or other characteristics.
Likewise, our field experiments could not control for background factors that may interact with treatment conditions in ways that 
are not directly observable (Lynch, 1982, 1983). For instance, particular facial attributes in the portraits could interact with occu­
pational labels in ways that influence responses. Although we held portraits constant within field experiments and implemented 
stimulus sampling in the survey experiment, our findings should be interpreted as robust to the sampled portraits rather than invariant 
to all possible facial variations. Replication across designs and the use of multiple photos in the survey experiment partially mitigate 
this concern (Dipboye, 1990). In addition, we caution that interpretation of the underlying mechanisms should be viewed as sug­
gestive. Although the estimated main effects are causally identified and generally consistent across the field and survey experiments,

their magnitudes are modest and most reach conventional levels of statistical significance. Similarly, bivariate effect-size estimates

Because some occupation-level contrasts involve smaller effective cell sizes, these results should be interpreted cautiously. The

Accordingly, we interpret the evidence regarding mechanisms and perceptual processes as suggestive of patterned differences in early-

Third, because our study was conducted in China, questions of contextual dependence arise. Although differences between Xi'an 
and Shanghai, as well as moderation by gender role attitude in the survey, are consistent with the possibility that local norms shape the 
magnitude of the romantic penalty, the two-city comparison does not permit strong inference about underlying sources of hetero­
geneity. Multiple contextual factors, including labor market conditions, perceptions and types of entrepreneurs, and platform usage 
patterns may co-vary with gender role attitudes across regions (He et al., 2019; Tonoyan et al., 2010). Replication in other institutional 
and cultural contexts would therefore be valuable.
Furthermore, several additional avenues for research remain open. Although we focus on online dating, it is unclear whether 
similar patterns would emerge in offline or network-based meeting contexts. Future research could also examine whether female 
entrepreneurs strategically select into particular dating channels or adjust self-presentation in response to perceived penalties. More 
broadly, understanding how occupational identity shapes relationship formation over the life course remains an important direction 
for further study.
5.4. Conclusion
Using field experiments, we provide causal evidence that a woman's entrepreneur status influences men's response behavior in 
early-stage online dating contexts. On average, female entrepreneur profiles receive fewer responses than comparable non- 
entrepreneur profiles, indicating an early-stage romantic penalty. This penalty, however, is mitigated among male entrepreneurs. 
Patterns observed in the supplementary survey experiment are consistent with the possibility that gendered trait inferences—and, to a 
lesser extent, concerns about work-related time demand—shape these evaluation patterns. By documenting this early-stage romantic 
penalty and its associated perceptions, our study advances female entrepreneurship research by highlighting obstacles women face in 
the family domain, and extends the family embeddedness perspective in entrepreneurial settings by demonstrating how entrepreneur 
status has consequences for family formation.
